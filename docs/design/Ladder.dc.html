How this report is made

Everything above comes from one phone camera on a tripod. That sets real limits, and it is better that you know them than that you trust a number too far.

How much of the picture we can measure
50%

This is how much of the full seven-category picture we can measure from one camera — not how sure we are of your rating. It is around half mainly because shot quality — pace, spin, how high a dink floats — is not measured yet. The counts we do report are checked against shot-by-shot review.

Where the numbers come from

Your position is read from your front foot, during live rallies only. Standing about between points is excluded.

Where the ball landed comes from detecting the bounce itself. 418 shots were played in your rallies; 86 of them were volleys and 60 were serves, and neither of those follows a bounce. That leaves 272 shots that should each have one — against 277 bounces detected, so the two line up.

Court covered is a work-rate figure, 221 ft per minute of play. On its own it is neither good nor bad — strong players often move less but get to better spots — so it is not rated.

Every shot count is shots played during rallies. A further 75 contacts were detected outside any rally — warm-up hits, balls picked up between points, and some false detections — and are excluded on purpose.

What we cannot see yet

Ball speed. At this distance the ball moves further between frames than we can resolve, so pace, spin and put-away power are absent from every category.

The paddle. Too small and too fast to track from here. Where the report says “paddle up” it is really measuring your hand.

Exactly where a point ends — and so, unforced errors. Which player ended a rally, and why, is right 8 times in 23, and the commonest mix-up is a ball into the net read as a ball nobody could return. Unforced errors are one of the clearest things separating 3.0 from 3.5 from 5.0 on the ladder above, and they are missing from your rating because of it. That is a hole in your number, not a judgement that errors do not matter — and it is the single most valuable thing left to fix. It is also why winners and errors are shown as a shape to notice rather than a scoreline.

How the seven categories add up to one number

Each category is given a fixed share of the rating — strategy 20%, third shot 18%, dink 15%, volley 13%, serve/return 12%, forehand 12%, backhand 10%. Those shares are a judgement about what matters at rec level, not something published by USA Pickleball.

Then each share is scaled by how much of that category we could actually measure, so a category we can barely see cannot drag your number around. Today that means strategy is carrying 39% of your rating rather than 20%, because positioning is the one thing we measure almost completely. The six ball-dependent categories are each measured on a third to a half of what they cover, and pull back toward the middle accordingly.

The practical reading: your number is a firm verdict on where you stood and a softer one on what you did with the ball. Both halves tighten as ball tracking improves.

What the level numbers are, and are not

The level descriptions are a condensed reading of the published USA Pickleball definitions across the seven categories. The thresholds behind your numbers are considered heuristics anchored to those definitions — they are not an official rating, and no official rating can be issued from a video. Treat this as a coaching instrument: the direction and the gaps are the useful part, the decimal place is not.
