Your improvement plan

Toward USAPA 4.5: Next USAPA half-step. Closing the focus-area gaps below moves the user toward 4.5.

Third Shot

You reached the kitchen line after 3 of your 7 third shots (43%), so you get up about half the time. The third shot is what buys that trip; if you are not making it, the drop is not doing its job.

A reliable third-shot drop is what lets your team get off the baseline and up to the net — the classic 3.0-to-4.0 gate.

Get-to-the-line: After every return, sprint to the NVZ line and freeze before the next ball — 'get to the line, then play.'
Third-shot-drop reps: From the baseline, practice dropping the 3rd ball softly into the kitchen so you can follow it to the net (drop vs drive depends on the return).

Volley

About 20% of your shots are volleys at the net; controlling and resetting them is what keeps net exchanges in your favour.

Punch volleys, blocks, and resets at the net decide the fast exchanges; controlling them keeps you on offense without overhitting.

Volley exchanges: Net-height volley-to-volley exchanges with a partner; soft hands, paddle in front.
Reset under pressure: Have a partner feed hard balls; block them softly into the kitchen instead of swinging.

Strategy

You're at the kitchen line about 37% of each rally, so there's room to be up at the line for more of each point; you and your partner are rarely at the line together at once (25% of the rally) At the kitchen your paddle sits low (around the waist) — carrying it up in front at the net lets you react to the fast exchanges.

Strategy is where points are won: getting up to the kitchen line, holding it with your partner, covering the court, and keeping the ball in play until you get one you can attack.

Get-to-the-line: After every return, sprint to the NVZ line and freeze before the next ball — 'get to the line, then play.'
Move as a unit: Shadow your partner across the kitchen keeping ~8-10 ft spacing; close the middle together.
Paddle up & ready: Between shots, hold the paddle up in front of you around chest height (not down at your side) so you can react to either wing without a wind-up.

Serve / Return

About 0% of your serves are faults, so your serve is reliable.

A deep, in-play serve and return start the point on your terms and deny the opponent easy offense.

Pre-serve routine: Same toss and contact point every serve; groove a repeatable routine.

Backhand

On your backhand, you hit 18 of 26 in front of your hip (69%); 4 of 10 drives had the right knee bend (40%). Meeting the ball in front of your hip from a loaded stance is the clean-technique base; pace and depth aren't measured yet.

A steady backhand you can drive, dink, and reset with takes away the side opponents most like to target.

Loaded stance: Play from a lower, athletic base — bend the knees so you get UNDER dinks and resets; power shots need less bend but still an athletic base, not straight legs.

  5. A shot played off the bounce follows exactly one bounce. Serves do not — a serve is struck from the hand — and neither do volleys, taken out of the air. Part of any shortfall is not a missing bounce at all: where we read a volley as a ground shot we expect a bounce that never existed, and on the clips with shot-by-shot review that accounts for about half of it. The rest thins the landing map and depth statistics, but does not affect your positioning or rating.
  6. Every shot count in this report is shots played during rallies. 77 further contacts were detected outside any rally — warm-up hits, balls picked up and returned between points, and some false detections — and are excluded, because they are not shots played in a point.
